Many small businesses start with a random logo made in Canva, then order business cards with a different font, and post graphics in completely different colours on Facebook. The result? The business looks amateurish, even if the service is great.

Brand identity is the answer to that problem. And it isn't reserved for corporations.

Brand identity is a set of rules deciding how your business looks everywhere: logo, colours, typefaces and the rules for using them. What exactly makes it different from a logo and what it should include, I described in [brand identity for a business](/en/blog/brand-identity-cost).

This guide is about something else: in what order to order it all, so you don't spend money on things that won't give you anything at this stage. Prices in PLN, net.

## Why the order matters

The most common scenario in a small business goes like this: business cards first, because they were needed for a trade fair. Then a flyer from another contractor, in a slightly different shade. Then a website where the logo is already in its fourth version.

Each of these materials is fine on its own. Together they add up to nothing, and after a year they all have to be redone. That's why the order can matter more than how much you spend.

## Where to start: step by step

### Step 1: Decide who you are and who you work for

Before a designer draws anything, you need to answer three questions:

- Who are your customers? (age, industry, expectations)
- How are you different from the competition?
- What word would you use to describe your business? (e.g. reliable, modern, local, exclusive)

It isn't philosophy: it's the foundation for every design decision.

### Step 2: Start with the logo

The logo is the starting point for the whole identity. A good designer will make it work in black and white, at small and large sizes, on light and dark backgrounds.

Avoid logos that are too complicated: at small sizes (favicon, stamps) they become unreadable.

### Step 3: Set the colour palette

Colour is the strongest element in building recognition. 2–3 colours are enough: a main colour, a supporting colour and a neutral (white or black).

Important: colours should be written as HEX (web), CMYK (print) and RGB (screens). Without these codes every printer will reproduce the colour differently.

### Step 4: Choose fonts and stick to them

One heading font, one body font, and no exceptions. That's enough. More fonts means visual chaos.

### Step 5: Gather the rules in a brand book

A brand book is a document describing how to use the logo, colours and typefaces. With it everyone (the print shop, the developer, whoever runs your social media) knows how your business should look and doesn't have to call to ask.

This step only makes sense when more than one person makes your materials. Before that, a file with your colours and the logo in a few versions is enough.

## What to do first on a small budget

The order that works for your first spending on how your business looks:

**1. A logo and two colours.** Without them, every following material will need fixing anyway.

**2. A business card and an email signature.** Two items you use every day that cost nothing to maintain.

**3. What you actually use.** A flyer, if you hand out flyers. Social media templates, if you actually post there. Not the other way round.

**4. A brand book.** Only when several people work on your materials.

Not every business needs a 60-page brand book straight away. For a small business this is enough:

## The most common mistakes small businesses make

- **Making the logo yourself in Canva:** no vectors, no originality, often infringing rights to the elements
- **Changing colours and fonts with every new material:** kills recognition
- **No source files:** when the designer disappears, you can't edit the design
- **Starting with business cards without a logo:** then everything has to be redone

## FAQ

**Where do I start building a brand identity?**

By deciding who the business is and who it's talking to; only then is the logo made. The order is: positioning, logo, colour palette, typography, and finally writing down the rules. Starting with a logo without that first step ends with a mark that needs fixing after a year.

**What materials does a new business need?**

To start: a logo in several versions, business cards, an email signature and a Google Business Profile. In the first months, a simple website and email on your business domain. The rest (flyers, sign, social templates) you add when they're actually needed. With me a logo, website and business cards together in the Firma w Internecie package cost 3,490 PLN.

**What's enough to begin with?**

A logo in three versions, two colours written as codes for screen and print, one typeface and a business card. With that set a small business functions fine for the first year, and every following material has something to refer to.

**How much does brand identity for a small business cost?**

A logo in three versions starts at 890 PLN, a logo with a mini brand book is 1,190 PLN, and the Marka Start package (logo, brand book, 150 printed business cards and an email signature) is 1,490 PLN. Offers under 300 PLN for a full identity are practically always a generator template, with no vectors and no copyright transfer.

**Does a small business really need brand identity?**

It needs consistency, not a thick document. A logo in a few variants, two or three colours and two typefaces used consistently are enough. That already sets apart a business that looks serious from one where every material looks different.

**How many colours should a brand identity have?**

Usually two main ones plus one accent and a neutral base: white and black or grey. More colours make consistency harder, because with every new material you have to decide again which one to use.

**In what order should I order print materials?**

Business cards first, because you use them with every contact. Then what follows from your industry: a flyer if you hand them to customers, a folder if you submit offers on paper, stickers if you leave equipment behind. Shop sign and van wrap last: they're the most expensive and hardest to replace.

**What should a brand book include?**

Logo variants and the rules for using them, minimum sizes, clear space, a colour palette with CMYK and RGB values, typefaces and examples of use. Plus a list of don'ts, what you must not do with the logo: often the most useful page of the whole document.
