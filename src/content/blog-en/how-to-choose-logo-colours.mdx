You don't choose logo colours based on what you like. You choose them for the places your logo will live: on a van, on a T-shirt, on a stamp, on a customer's phone. The owner's favourite colour is a fine place to start. It's a bad place to finish.

Here's a typical case. Someone has a green and gold logo with a drop shadow in mind, because it looked great on screen. Then it turns out that on a black and white invoice the mark melts into a grey blob, and embroidery on fleeces costs more because every colour is a separate thread.

Below are five rules you can check yourself before you talk to a designer.

## How to choose logo colours: start with places, not a palette

Take a sheet of paper and write down where the logo will actually appear. Not "everywhere". Be specific.

- The side of a van or a tarp on a building site.
- A business card and a stamp.
- T-shirts, fleeces, caps.
- A Facebook profile picture, which means a small circle.
- An invoice printed on an ordinary office printer.

That list tells you what you need. A construction firm whose logo lives on tarps and hard hats needs strong contrast you can see from a distance. A clinic that mostly uses its website and Instagram can afford softer shades.

## Rule 1. One colour, two at most

The logos that work best have one main colour and maybe one extra. A third colour rarely adds anything, and it makes everything else harder.

## Rule 2. The logo has to work in black and white

This is the simplest test. Print your logo in greyscale on an ordinary printer. Then make a white version on a dark background.

If the mark stops being readable, the colours are doing all the work for it. And there will be no colour on a stamp, an engraving, an invoice or a newspaper ad. That's why every logo I design also comes in a single-colour version.

## Rule 3. Check the contrast

Pale yellow text on white, grey on navy, red on green. All of these are hard to see from a few metres away. And some people with colour vision deficiency can't tell red from green at all.

An easy way to check: step three metres back from the monitor, or shrink the logo to the size of an app icon on a phone. If you have to squint, the contrast is too weak.

## Rule 4. Colour costs money in print and on clothing

The number of colours affects the price of printing. You pay for it with every order, for years.

- **Screen printing on T-shirts:** each colour needs its own screen and its own pass. A four-colour logo costs more than a one-colour one. I compare the methods in [DTF or screen printing](/en/blog/dtf-or-screen-printing-t-shirts).
- **Embroidery:** each colour is a separate thread. You can't embroider a gradient or a shadow.
- **Vinyl for vans and shop windows:** letters are cut from vinyl in a single colour. Tonal transitions need a full print instead.
- **Printing on paper:** a colour on screen and the same colour on paper look different. Bright green or neon pink from a screen comes out duller in standard print. I explain why in [CMYK vs RGB](/en/blog/cmyk-vs-rgb).

So the mark itself is better off with fewer colours and flat fills, without shadows or gradients.

## Rule 5. See which colours your competitors use

Search Google for your trade and town, for example "plumber Rzeszów". Take a screenshot of the results and the map. Look at which colours keep coming up.

You have two options. You can stick to the colour typical for your trade, so customers know right away what you do. Or you can pick a different one on purpose, so you stand out from five businesses in the same colour. Both are fine, as long as it's a decision and not an accident.

## Colours written down

Once the colours are chosen, they need to be written down as numbers: separately for print (CMYK), for the web (HEX, RGB) and, if needed, for exact ink matching (Pantone). Without that, every print shop and every T-shirt supplier matches the colour by eye. Two years later you have three shades of navy and none of them is yours.

## FAQ

**How many colours should a logo have?**

One main colour and one extra at most. Plus a single-colour version, always: black and white. More colours push up the cost of embroidery, screen printing and vehicle wraps.

**Can I have a logo with a gradient?**

You can, but only as an extra version, for example for the website. The main version should use flat colours, because you can't make a gradient with embroidery, vinyl or a stamp.

**Why does my logo colour look different in print than on screen?**

A screen emits light, paper reflects it. A screen can show more vivid colours than ink can reproduce. That's why logo colours are written down separately for print and for the web.

**Can I change the colours in a logo I already have?**

Yes. If the mark itself is good, a refresh is often enough: tidying up the colours, adding a single-colour version and writing down the palette. You don't have to start from scratch.
