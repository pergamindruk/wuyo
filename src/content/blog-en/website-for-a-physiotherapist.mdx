A website for a physiotherapist has to answer one question, the one a patient is asking at night with an aching back: can this clinic help me, and when can I come in?

This is for physiotherapists who run their own practice or do home visits. The phone rings in the middle of a session, patients keep asking the same things, and the clinics ranking above you in Google simply have a decent website.

## Who looks for a physiotherapist on Google

Usually someone with a specific problem. They type “physiotherapist Rzeszów”, “knee rehab after surgery Rzeszów” or “manual therapy for back pain”. They open three or four results and compare them.

That person wants to know:

1. Do you treat my problem?
2. Who will be treating me, and what training do they have?
3. How much is a visit, and how long does it take?
4. When is the next free slot, and how do I book?

## A website for a physiotherapist: what it must include

Every point on this list is a question the patient will ask on the phone anyway.

<CheckItem>The problems you treat, in the patient's words: back pain, knee after ligament reconstruction, shoulder, rehab after a stroke</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>Types of visit: consultation, manual therapy, massage, home visit. The length of each one</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>Visit prices as text, not in a PDF or an image</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>Your photo and a few lines about you: degree, courses, licence number</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>What to bring to the first visit: test results, the MRI report, comfortable clothes</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>Address, map and directions. Is there a lift, can you drive up to the entrance</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>Opening hours and a booking button right at the top</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>A link to the reviews on your Google Business Profile</CheckItem>

Two of these do the most work. The first is the list of problems. A patient isn't searching for “soft tissue therapy”, they want someone who can fix their shoulder pain. The second is your photo and background. People go to a physiotherapist with a body that hurts, so they want to know who they're going to see.

Don't overpromise. In Poland a physiotherapy practice counts as a healthcare provider, and information about its services can't read like advertising. So on a clinic website I keep it factual: what you treat and what a visit looks like. No “we'll fix it in three sessions”, and no hand-picked patient praise on the home page. The physiotherapists' code of professional ethics also has rules on how to present your services.

## A separate page for each problem

Someone searching “shoulder rehab Rzeszów” will be shown a page about shoulders by Google, not a general “physiotherapy clinic” page. That's why I recommend a site with several pages to clinics that treat a range of problems. One for the spine, one for rehab after injuries and surgery, one for home visits.

Each page covers what patients come in with, what the therapy involves, how long it usually takes, what a visit costs and answers to common questions. I explained the difference between one page and several in [one-page or multi-page website](/en/blog/one-page-or-multi-page-website).

If you work alone and mostly do one thing, for example home visits for older patients, a one-page site is enough.

## Online booking without collecting health data

You have a patient on the table and the phone rings for the third time. Everyone wants to know about free slots.

If you already take bookings through a booking system for clinics, nothing needs to change. I put a clear “Book a visit” button on the site that goes straight to your calendar.

If you don't have one, a simple form is enough:

- type of visit (chosen from a list),
- the day and time that suit them,
- name and phone number.

There's deliberately no “describe your symptoms” field. Health information is a special category of personal data, and a website form isn't a good place to collect it. You'll get the details on the phone or at the first visit. You prepare the privacy policy yourself, from a template or with a lawyer. I put it on the site and link it to the form.

## Your Google profile and website work together

Someone who types “physiotherapist Rzeszów” first sees a map with three clinics. On the map, reviews and a complete profile win. Below the map are the websites. A clinic that only has a profile on a booking platform won't show up on the map or in the website results.

What works best is the full set: a Google Business Profile with reviews and photos of the clinic, a link from it to a website with your problems and prices, and a booking button on the site. Hours and prices have to match everywhere. I covered setting up the profile in [Google Business Profile for a small business](/en/blog/google-business-profile-small-business).

## FAQ

**How much does a website for a physiotherapist cost?**

With me, a one-page site is from 1,990 PLN net and a business site with up to 5 pages is 2,990 PLN net. Add 23% VAT. The domain and hosting for the first year are included.

**Should I put visit prices on the website?**

Yes. It's one of the first things patients ask. If the price depends on the length of the visit or travel, give a “from” price and say what it depends on.

**I have a profile on a booking platform. Why do I need a website?**

A website gets you into Google results for specific problems with your town's name, and gives patients one place to read about you, your prices and how to get there. The booking profile stays; the website just points to it.

**What should I prepare before ordering a website?**

A list of the problems you treat, visit prices, opening hours, a few lines about yourself, photos of the clinic and a link to your Google profile. The full list is in [what to prepare for a website](/en/blog/what-to-prepare-for-a-website).
