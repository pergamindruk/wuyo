That half hour pays for itself. The brief decides whether the first design lands close to the target, or whether you and the designer spend three weeks guessing what you meant.

Nobody can design a logo that is "modern but classic, simple but rich, and liked by everyone". A designer needs a direction. The brief gives them one and saves you rounds of revisions.

Below is what to put in it, step by step, whether you're ordering a logo, a website or a full brand identity.

## What a brief is

A brief is a short description of the job, written before the first line is drawn. It answers a few questions:

- Who are you and what do you do?
- Who are you talking to?
- What should this project change?
- How do you want people to see your business?
- What do you definitely not want?
- What are your limits: budget, time, format?

<Callout type="info">
A brief isn't a letter to a government office. Write the way you talk. Be honest and specific. 1–3 A4 pages are enough.
</Callout>

## A logo brief: what to include

### 1. Business and industry
- The full business or brand name.
- What exactly you do. Not "construction services" but "turnkey flat renovations".
- How long you've been trading: a new business or one with history.
- Whether you already have any materials or an old logo.

### 2. Your customer
- Who buys from you: age, industry, businesses or private clients.
- Where they find you: Google, referrals, Facebook, walking past your premises.
- What they look at when choosing: price, deadline, reviews, appearance.

### 3. Your business's character
- Three adjectives that describe you, e.g. reliable, calm, fast.
- If your business were a person: how would it dress and talk?
- What should a customer feel when they see the logo?

### 4. What you like
- Colours you like and colours you don't want.
- Style: simple, traditional, luxury, technical, handmade.
- Lettering: classic with serifs, or plain and modern.
- 3–5 logos you like, with one sentence on why.
- 3–5 logos you don't like, and why. This often says more than the first list.

### 5. Competition
- Who does the same thing in your area?
- How do you want to differ from them?
- Do you want to look like the rest of the industry to inspire trust, or deliberately different?

### 6. Where the logo will live
- Website, business cards, shop sign, van, workwear, packaging, social media.
- Whether it will sit on dark backgrounds, light ones or both.
- Which files you want. If you're not sure, read [which logo files you should get from a designer](/en/blog/logo-files-from-designer).

### 7. Deadline and budget
- When do you need the finished logo?
- How much do you want to spend? Even roughly.
- Is there a key date: opening, a trade fair, a product launch?

## A website brief: extra questions

A website has more moving parts than a logo, so there are a few more questions about its purpose, content and technical side.

**The website's purpose:**
- What should the site do: collect enquiries, show your work, sell?
- What should a visitor do: call, fill in a form, buy?
- Is it a new site, or does it replace an old one?

**Content:**
- Which sections or pages do you need: services, portfolio, about, contact?
- Do you have the text ready, or does it need writing?
- Do you have your own photos, or do they need taking or buying?
- Do you want to edit the content yourself in an admin panel?
- One language or several?

**Technical:**
- Do you already have a domain and hosting?
- Where should form enquiries go?
- Does the site need to connect to anything else: analytics, a newsletter, a shop?

The list of materials worth having before a website project starts is in [what to prepare for a website](/en/blog/what-to-prepare-for-a-website).

## Mistakes that drag a project out

<Callout type="warning">
**The most common one:** "I want a logo that's modern, classic and minimalist all at once."

Those wishes cancel each other out. Pick one direction. Show 2–3 examples and say: "something closer to this".
</Callout>

<GreenRed
  green="Good: 'I want a simple logo, I like Muji, Aesop, Veja. Black or navy. No gradients, no gold, no complicated drawings.'"
  red="Bad: 'I want a logo that's modern but classic, simple but rich. Something everyone will like.'"
/>

**Other mistakes I see most often:**

- A one-line brief: "a logo for a construction company". The designer has to guess the rest.
- No examples, good or bad.
- "Needed by next week", sent on a Friday evening.
- No budget. The designer doesn't know whether to propose something simple or something bigger.
- Changing the basics after the first design. "We're changing the name after all" means starting again.

## Can't write a brief? That's fine

With a first project it's normal. You have three options.

**A conversation.** A good designer will walk you through the questions. Fifteen to thirty minutes of talking replaces an evening of writing.

**A short version.** Answer three questions: what you do, who for, and what you like. We'll work out the rest along the way.

## FAQ

**Where can I download a design brief template?**

Free, the link comes to your email: [brief template PDF](/pobierz/brief-dla-projektanta). It has 3 A4 pages, questions for a logo and a website, and a checklist before sending. The template is in Polish.

**What is a design brief?**

A short description of the job: what your business does, what the project is for, who it should reach and what you expect from it. The clearer you are, the fewer revisions later.

**How many pages should a brief have?**

1–3 pages. Being specific matters, not length. You're not writing a thesis.

**Can I show examples instead of writing a brief?**

Yes. Examples of logos, websites and colours help a lot. Show what you dislike too. Bad examples tell a designer as much as good ones.

**Do I need a brief for a small job like a business card?**

For a business card a short version is enough: industry, colours, style, the details to print. A full brief matters most for a logo, a brand identity and a website.

**How do I write a brief if I don't know what I want?**

Answer three questions: what your business does, who your customer is, and show 2–3 designs you like. That's enough for a first conversation. The designer will draw out the rest with you.

**How long does a brief take?**

The first time, 30–60 minutes of calm filling in. Later briefs for the same business take 10–15 minutes, because most answers don't change.
