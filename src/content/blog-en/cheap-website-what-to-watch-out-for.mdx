This article isn't about not buying a cheap website. It's about knowing what you pay for. Below are 7 things you can check in the offer or with one question to the contractor.

## 1. Who the domain is registered to

This is the most important question in the whole article. The domain is your business's address on the internet. If it's registered to the contractor, it formally belongs to them.

As long as everything's fine, there's no problem. The problem comes when you want to switch website providers, or the contractor stops answering the phone. Then you can lose an address that has been on your business cards, van wrap and Google Maps for years.

**Ask directly:** "Will the domain be registered to my business?" The only good answer is "yes".

## 2. What the second year costs

Many cheap offers are only cheap in the first year. Then the invoice arrives for the server, domain and "service", often higher than you expected.

Ask for the amount in writing: what will I pay in the second year and after? If the contractor can't tell you, that's an answer too.

With me, from the second year you can maintain the site yourself, or take Website Care for 199 PLN net a month: server, domain, backups, security and an hour of small changes a month. The full breakdown of costs is in [how much does website maintenance cost](/en/blog/website-maintenance-cost).

## 3. Is the website yours, or just rented

Some cheap websites are built in subscription site builders. You pay every month, and when you stop, the site disappears. You can't move it to another company, because there's nothing to move.

That doesn't have to be bad. But then it's renting, not buying. Compare it with offers where you get the files and full rights after paying.

## 4. A template or a design made for your business

A ready template is quick and cheap. The trouble is that hundreds of other businesses use the same template, often in the same industry.

A customer comparing three local builders sees three similar websites. They choose the one that looks more serious. Or the one with better photos of finished work.

Ask for 2–3 websites the contractor has made before. Open them on your phone. If they look identical, you know what you'll get.

## 5. Has anyone thought about Google

A website nobody finds is like a shop sign in a basement. Cheap websites often lack the basics: page titles for local phrases ("plumber Rzeszów"), a description for Google, fast loading on phones.

You don't need to understand any of this. Just ask: "What will you do so the website shows up in Google for my services in my town?" If you hear vague answers, keep looking.

You can check a finished website yourself in 10 minutes. I describe how in [how to check whether your business website is losing customers](/en/blog/is-your-business-website-losing-customers).

## 6. Who will add your new price list in six months

Every business changes: a new number, a new service, new opening hours. With cheap websites this is a common moment of disappointment. Every small change is charged separately, or nobody does it.

Ask how much a small change costs and how quickly it'll be done. With me an hour of changes a month is part of Website Care, so you don't call for a quote with every correction.

## 7. Does the form actually work

It sounds trivial, but it happens a lot. The contact form looks fine, but the messages never arrive anywhere. Or they land in spam.

After the website is handed over, send yourself a message through the form. And call from your phone by tapping the number on the site. Two minutes that save you lost enquiries.

## When a cheap website makes sense

If you're just starting, customers come mainly through recommendations and the site is only meant to be a business card with a phone number, a simple, cheap website is enough. What matters is that the domain is yours and you know what the second year will cost.

If the website is meant to bring enquiries from Google and your service costs several thousand, saving on the website is usually the most expensive saving in the business.
