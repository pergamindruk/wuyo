A website development contract has to answer three questions: what exactly you get, who owns the domain and the site, and what happens when the work together ends. If the contract is silent on any of these, the problem will show up a year or two later. Usually when the developer stops answering the phone.

The story I hear most often goes like this. A business pays for a website, everything works, and three years later it turns out the domain is registered to the developer. Without them, nothing can be changed or moved. One line in the contract would have prevented it.

Below are eight clauses worth checking before you sign. You don't need to know anything about websites to find them.

## What a website development contract must include

The contract doesn't need ten pages. Two are enough, as long as they're specific. Go through them one by one.

### 1. Scope: how many pages and what's on them

"A business website" means nothing. The contract, or the offer it refers to, should list:

<CheckItem>the number of pages, e.g. home, about, services, projects, contact</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>what's on the site: a contact form, a map, a gallery, a mobile version</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>who writes the copy and where the photos come from</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>what's outside the scope and how it will be priced before anyone starts on it</CheckItem>

The last point matters most. Without it, every extra page turns into a tense conversation about money.

### 2. Domain and hosting registered to your business

This is the clause that most often saves businesses from trouble. The domain is your website's address, and hosting is the server the site runs on. Both accounts should be set up in your business's name, even if the developer pays for them.

### 3. Copyright in the design

The site's visual design, copy written by the developer and photos from a shoot are all protected by copyright. Under Polish law, transferring the economic rights to you requires a written contract that lists the fields of use, meaning the ways you're allowed to use the design. Paying the invoice alone doesn't do it.

Look for a sentence saying that once you've paid in full, the developer transfers the economic copyright in the design to you, rather than just granting you a licence. The details, including how a licence differs from a transfer of rights, are in [logo copyright](/en/blog/logo-copyright). It works the same way for a website.

### 4. Licences for the theme, plugins and photos

Not everything on the site belongs to the developer. A theme, paid plugins and stock photos come with their own licences. The contract should say:

- who the paid licences are bought for (ideally you),
- who pays to renew them from the second year on,
- that the photos on the site are legal and where they come from.

This is where surprises tend to appear after a year: a plugin stops updating because the licence expired, and nobody knew it needed renewing.

### 5. A deadline counted from when materials arrive

A fair deadline in a contract doesn't say "website in two weeks". It says the deadline starts on the day the developer has all the materials, and each day of delay in delivering them moves the deadline by the same amount.

It sounds like protection for the developer, but it protects you too. You know straight away what you need to prepare and by when. The list of things to gather is in [what to prepare for a website](/en/blog/what-to-prepare-for-a-website), and how long the whole job realistically takes is in [how long does a website take](/en/blog/how-long-does-a-website-take).

### 6. Payments and revisions

The most common setup is a 50% deposit up front and the rest on handover. In the contract, also check:

<CheckItem>whether the amounts are net and whether 23% VAT is added on top</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>how many rounds of revisions are included, or whether revisions within scope continue until you approve</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>what counts as a revision and what counts as a change of scope (a new page is not a revision)</CheckItem>

### 7. Handover and what you get with the site

Handover is the moment the site becomes yours. The contract should say how many days you have to report issues and what you receive at handover:

- login details for the domain, hosting and the site's admin panel,
- an account you can use to change text or photos yourself, if that's what you agreed,
- a short training session, ideally recorded so you can come back to it in six months.

### 8. Care after launch and cancelling it

After launch a site needs updates, backups and domain renewals. If the developer handles this, the contract should say what the care plan covers, what it costs per month and how to cancel it.

The most important sentence in this part: if you cancel, the developer hands over full admin access and the latest copy of the site. Without it, "cancelling" means building the site again from scratch. What care usually costs is worked out in [website maintenance cost](/en/blog/website-maintenance-cost).

## Red flags in the contract, or in the lack of one

If you see any of these, ask before you sign:

- No contract, "we'll sort it out on Messenger".
- Domain and hosting in the developer's name, with nothing about handing them over.
- A licence to use the design instead of a transfer of rights.
- No list of pages, just "a business website".
- Mandatory care with no way to cancel, or a penalty for leaving.

## FAQ

**Does a website development contract have to be in writing?**

Under Polish law the contract for building the site itself can be agreed even by email. But a transfer of economic copyright in the design must be in writing, or it's invalid. So it's best to have the whole thing in writing.

**How do I check who my domain is registered to?**

Enter the address in a WHOIS lookup (for .pl domains it's run by NASK) and see who the registrant is. Details of private individuals are hidden, so if you don't see your business's name, ask the developer directly and request a screenshot from the registrar's panel.

**What if the developer won't hand over access to the site?**

First check what the contract says. If it has a clause about handing over access and rights, ask for them in writing. If there's no contract, try to agree now and sign a handover. If that doesn't work, talk to a lawyer.

**Is a ready-made contract template from the internet enough?**

As a starting point, yes, but go through it with the list from this article. Templates often skip the domain, plugin licences and the rules for cancelling care. For a bigger job, such as an online shop, it's worth showing the contract to a lawyer.
