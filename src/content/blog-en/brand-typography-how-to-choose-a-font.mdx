Brand typography is the answer to one question: what letters does your business use when it talks to customers? In the logo, on the business card, on invoices, on the website and on the side of the van. If each of those looks different, customers don't see one business. They see five.

A typical case. The logo has a nice rounded wordmark. Someone set the flyer in Arial because it was already on the computer. The website uses whatever font came with the template, and the Word quote is in Calibri. None of these is a mistake on its own. Put together, it looks accidental.

Below are a few rules you can check yourself before you order a logo or start tidying up your materials.

## Brand identity and typography: how they fit together

A visual identity is made of a few things: the logo, colours, fonts and rules for using them. Fonts are the least noticeable part, yet they show up most often. You see the logo once, at the top of the page. You read the letters on every line of every quote, price list and email.

That's why brand typography usually has two layers:

- **The lettering in the logo.** Often adjusted by hand: spacing fixed, letters trimmed, sometimes the whole word drawn from scratch. It's part of the mark, not a font for writing text.
- **Fonts for your materials.** One or two, used for headings and body text on business cards, flyers, the website and documents.

The two layers don't have to be identical. They have to suit each other. The bigger picture is in [brand identity: what it is and what it costs](/en/blog/brand-identity-cost).

## Rule 1. Two fonts are enough

One for headings, one for body text. Or a single family in a few weights: light, regular, bold. For a small business, that's plenty.

## Rule 2. Check Polish characters before you fall in love

Plenty of nice fonts online have no Polish letters. Then "Ż", "ł" or "ę" show up in a different typeface or as empty boxes. You won't notice when browsing, because the sample text is in English.

A simple test: type "Zażółć gęślą jaźń" into the font preview. That phrase contains every Polish character. If any letter looks out of place, keep looking.

The second thing is weights. A font with only one weight runs out fast. You'll miss a bold for prices, an italic for side notes and a light version for small print.

## Rule 3. A free font isn't always free for business

A font is software, and it comes with a licence. Some fonts on free download sites are for personal use only. A logo, a shop sign or T-shirts you sell all count as commercial use.

Safe choices:

- **Google Fonts.** The fonts use open licences that allow business use, in print and on websites.
- **Fonts bought with a licence.** The licence then says where you can use them: website, print, app.

If your designer uses a paid font, ask who holds the licence and whether it covers your materials. In the logo itself, the text is converted to outlines, so you don't need the font to use the finished logo. To write new flyers, you do. What else you should get from your designer is covered in [logo files from your designer](/en/blog/logo-files-from-designer).

## Rule 4. Readable when small and from a distance

A logo font looks good on a big screen. Then it ends up on a pen, a stamp or a profile picture on a phone.

Two tests you can do in five minutes:

- Shrink the logo and a bit of text to 2 cm wide. Print it. Can you read it without squinting?
- Write your business name in capitals on an A4 sheet and walk five metres away. That's how customers see your van or sign.

Thin, heavily decorative and slanted typefaces usually fail both tests. They can be made to work in a logo, but I don't set flyer text in them.

## Rule 5. The font's character suits your trade

Letters say something too. Blocky, heavy ones suggest strength and reliability, which suits construction or a garage. Light ones with serifs (the small strokes at the ends of letters) suggest tradition and calm: a law firm, an accounting office, a clinic. Rounded ones feel friendly: a nursery, a café, a toy shop.

These aren't strict rules. They help you check that the font isn't saying something different from you. You can run the same test with colours, as described in [how to choose logo colours](/en/blog/how-to-choose-logo-colours).

## Write your fonts down

Once you've chosen your typography, write it down: typeface names, weights for headings and body text, sizes on business cards and in documents, and where to download the fonts. Without that, every print shop and every person in your business picks letters by eye.

Those rules go in a brand book, together with the colours and logo versions. What's in one is covered in [brand book: what it is and what it costs](/en/blog/brand-book-guidelines-cost).

## FAQ

**How many fonts should a business have?**

Two: one for headings, one for body text. It can also be a single family in a few weights. More than that usually makes a mess.

**Does the logo font have to match the website font?**

No. The lettering in a logo is often adjusted or drawn from scratch. The fonts on your website and materials should match its character, not be identical.

**Can I use a free font in my logo?**

Yes, if its licence allows commercial use. Google Fonts do. Fonts from random download sites are often personal use only, so check the licence before you decide.

**What if my font doesn't have Polish characters?**

Switch to one that does. Swapping single letters for another typeface always shows, especially in your business name or headings.
