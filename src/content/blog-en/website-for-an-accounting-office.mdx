A website for an accounting office has one job: to convince a business owner they can hand you their invoices and sleep at night. If your site doesn't tell them whether you handle lump-sum tax, roughly what it costs and how to get in touch, they'll call the office that did say.

This is for owners of small accounting offices and self-employed bookkeepers. You have regular clients from referrals, and you'd like calls from people who just typed “accounting office Rzeszów” into Google as well.

## Who looks for an accounting office on Google

Usually someone in one of three situations. They're starting a business and don't know where to begin. They're unhappy with their current accountant and want a change, typically before the end of the year. Or their business has grown: a first employee, VAT registration, a different form of taxation.

Each of them wants to know the same things, fast:

1. Do you work with businesses like mine?
2. Roughly how much is it a month?
3. How does switching from another accountant work?
4. Can I trust you with my money and my data?

## A website for an accounting office: what has to be on it

Start from this list. Each item answers a question the client would ask on the phone anyway.

<CheckItem>A list of services. Be specific: “revenue and expense ledger”, “lump-sum tax”, “full accounting”, “HR and payroll”, “ZUS settlements”, “help setting up a business”, not “accounting services”</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>Who you work for. Sole traders, companies, the industries you know best, for example construction or transport</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>Monthly price ranges, depending on the form of taxation and the number of documents</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>Who you are. A photo, your full name, how many years you've been keeping books</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>Your office's liability insurance, a certificate number or membership of an accountants' association, if you have one</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>Address, map, opening hours, and whether documents can be dropped off, emailed or uploaded to a program</CheckItem>
<CheckItem>A phone number at the very top that can be tapped on a phone</CheckItem>

In an accounting office, a photo of the person makes a big difference. The client is trusting you with the company's finances and their troubles with the tax office. They want to see who they'll be talking to.

## Should you publish prices?

Most offices are afraid to, because “every business is different”. That's true. But without any number, a client can't tell whether they can afford to talk to you, so they call somewhere that shows a price.

You don't need a price list down to the last zloty. Ranges are enough, based on what you check when quoting anyway:

- form of taxation (lump-sum, revenue and expense ledger, full accounting),
- number of documents a month,
- whether the business is a VAT payer,
- number of employees on contracts.

Add the sentence “I'll give you the exact price after a short conversation”. The people you'll mostly put off are the ones looking for the cheapest accountant in town.

## A quote form instead of three phone calls

A simple form on the site collects what you have to ask anyway. The client ticks their form of taxation and enters a rough number of invoices a month, the number of employees and whether they're on VAT. Then they leave a phone number.

The request lands in your inbox. You call back with a quote ready, instead of spending twenty minutes asking about the basics.

<Callout type="info">
Don't ask for a tax number or scanned documents in the form. At this stage, the form of taxation, the number of documents and a phone number are enough. The shorter the form, the more people send it.
</Callout>

## A few short articles that bring clients from Google

Business owners type questions into Google: “lump-sum or tax scale”, “how to change accounting office”, “what is KSeF”. An office that answers these in plain language on its own site has a better chance of appearing in the results, and it immediately looks like it knows its stuff.

You don't have to blog every week. Three or four good articles about what clients ask you most often will keep working for years. Especially one about switching offices: step by step, what the client has to do and what you'll handle for them.
